# Roundel design context

## Product

A personal, instrument-first practice tool for learning harmony at the piano, called **Roundel**. The twelve keys are laid out as a transit network — stations on lines — which is how you navigate and how the curriculum is built; the circle-of-fifths wheel remains for exploring chords and scales. It combines that network, MIDI input, ear training, spaced repetition, and generated backing tracks. It is a focused tool for one practising musician, not a social product or a generic music-learning platform.

## Users and jobs

- A pianist at a physical keyboard, often with both hands occupied.
- The primary job is to begin useful practice quickly and stay in musical flow.
- Secondary jobs are exploring harmonic relationships, diagnosing voicings, and configuring a persistent personal setup.
- The interface may sit on a music stand at arm's length, so important controls and feedback must remain legible and tappable.

## Personality

Focused, tactile, musically literate, and quietly opinionated. It should feel like a well-made practice instrument: calm enough not to compete with the music, but distinctive through pitch colour, precise typography, and direct language.

## Visual language

- Dark near-neutral ground; the twelve pitch-class colours carry meaning and most of the chroma.
- Transit-map vocabulary: keys are stations (a ring or disc in the tonic's colour), curriculum steps are lines (drawn in ink and weight, never hue), a workout is a departure. The brand mark is the Roundel — a ring with a bar across it, the ring being the circle of fifths — in `BrandMark.svelte`.
- Space Grotesk for expressive musical labels and IBM Plex Mono for degrees, timing, metadata, and compact controls.
- Flat, structurally clear surfaces with restrained borders and shadows.
- Rounded corners are modest and consistent. Avoid ornamental cards, excessive pills, gradients, glass effects, and decorative motion.
- Motion explains state changes only and must respect reduced-motion preferences.

## Interaction principles

- Keep the default path short; reveal the full choice set progressively.
- Preserve hands-free controls through sustain pedal and plain Space, without intercepting form controls or buttons.
- Audio begins only from a clear user gesture, then may continue automatically during a drill.
- Make destructive actions confirmable and persistence failures recoverable.
- Use native controls and visible focus. The on-screen keyboard must have a real computer-keyboard equivalent.
- Design mobile layouts deliberately, while retaining the landscape music-stand experience.

## Copy

Use plain, compact, musician-friendly language. Explain unfamiliar timing terms where they appear. Avoid generic dashboard language, inflated claims, and vague success or error messages.
